Mapping the Unseen

Surfaces of rocky planets host features, like pits and skylights, that may lead to subsurface voids.
These structures are scientifically valuable and operationally strategic for future exploration.

Opportunity Meets Urgency

Decades of orbital missions have produced petabytes of imagery and radar data
over the Moon and Mars rich, archives still underexploited.

Meanwhile, space agencies are preparing for a sustained human return to the Moon
and the first crewed missions to Mars.

Identifying stable, accessible shelters is becoming a strategic priority.

4 - Subsurface Sounding Radar Cavity Detection

Can orbital subsurface-sounding radar reliably expose hidden cavities beneath or near skylights?

Characterization of a lava plain NW of Ascraeus Mons, Mars, through surface morphometric analysis and SHARAD subsurface detections

Submitted on Journal of Geophisical Research: Planets Journal (AGU)

Currently 1st round of reviews

Study Area & Data

Shallow lava plain NW of Ascraeus Mons; used high‑resolution DEMs and SHARAD radar tracks.


Credits: Nodjoumi et al, 2025

Morphometric Analysis

Mapped surface slopes, roughness, and pit chains; identified flow boundaries and channel infill.


Credits: Nodjoumi et al, 2025

SHARAD Processing

EchoTerraeTrace for multi‑track 3D radar echo comparisons across orbital overpasses.


Credits: Nodjoumi et al, 2025

Radar Detections

Recovered subsurface reflectors at 5–20 m depth, correlating with possible distinct flow units. Cluster analysis


Credits: Nodjoumi et al, 2025

Stratigraphic Scenarios

Proposed layering: sequence of lava flows intercalated with pyroclastic products from localized minor vents, and/or glacial sediments


Credits: Nodjoumi et al, 2025

Caveats & Limitations

Resolution limits (<15 m), radar clutter from rough terrain, and dielectric property uncertainties.

Implications

Evidence for complex intercalated layers, with consistent lateral continuity; validates EchoTerraeTrace for future Martian subsurface studies.

Deep Learning Architectures

🧠 What is a CNN?

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is an algorithm that takes in an input image, assigns importance to various aspects/objects, and learns how to differentiate among them. It is composed mainly of Convolution Layers, Pooling Layers, and Fully Connected Layers in different configurations.

Nodjoumi et al., 2022

Convolution Layers: Specific kernels/filters used to reduce the complexity of an image into high-level features (e.g., edges, color, gradient orientation).

Kernel / Filter:

  1 0 1
  0 1 0
  1 0 1

Pooling Layers: Reduce the spatial size of the convolved features. Useful for reducing computation and extracting dominant features. E.g., max pooling.

Geology of Mars

  1. ~4.6 Ga origin, Noachian aqueous erosion → valley networks & outflow channels
  2. Hesperian volcanism built Tharsis (Olympus Mons, Arsia Mons…) & Syrtis Major
  3. Thin CO₂ atmosphere (–63 °C avg.), 0.38 g → aeolian sculpting of volcanoes, canyons, basins
  4. Global geologic map released in 2014 (Tanaka et al. 2014)

Geology of the Moon

  1. Highlands of ferroan‑anorthosite from Lunar Magma Ocean; maria from 3.0‑4.1 Ga basaltic floods
  2. Rilles, pyroclastics, limited intrusive record → dominantly extrusive volcanism (Cruishank & Wood 1972)
  3. Giant‑impact origin → differentiated mantle, KREEP layer, plagioclase crust
